Google is known for coding interviews, but its loops also assess **leadership and how you work with others**. Candidates and prep guides commonly call this the "Googleyness & Leadership" interview. Google's own public material is helpful here. Its re:Work guide on structured interviewing explains the attributes Google assesses, the two kinds of question it asks, and how answers are scored with rubrics. That tells you exactly how to prepare.

<Callout type="note" title="Checked October 2026">

Google's public guidance has changed over the years; this page separates what Google says today from what it has said before and from what candidates report. Re-check the official pages before your interview.

</Callout>

## What Google says it assesses

Google's current re:Work guide ("A guide to structured interviewing") describes three attributes:

| Attribute | What it means | How it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role-related knowledge** | The skills and expertise the role needs, and how you've applied them | Technical rounds; your project deep dive |
| **Problem solving** | How you work through complex, ambiguous scenarios to a logical answer | Coding and design rounds, and hypothetical questions |
| **Leadership** | For individual contributors, **emergent leadership**: initiative and cross-team collaboration. For managers: empowering a team, long-term strategy, developing people | Behavioral and hypothetical questions |

Earlier Google material, including its former People Operations head Laszlo Bock's book *Work Rules!* (2015), described **four** attributes:

- general cognitive ability;
- leadership;
- role-related knowledge;
- **"Googleyness"**, often summarised as comfort with ambiguity, a bias for action and collaborative behaviour.

The name has stuck in prep materials, so expect the "Googleyness & Leadership" label even though Google's newest guide frames it as leadership.

## Two kinds of question

Google's guide describes both:

- **Behavioral**: "Tell me about a time your behaviour had a positive impact on your team", followed by questions about your goal, how teammates reacted, and what you did next.
- **Hypothetical**: a realistic future scenario. The guide's example: a competitor starts charging a small monthly fee for a product similar to yours; what do you do? Follow-ups probe the trade-offs, long-term sustainability and the organisational impact.

Answers are scored against **rubrics** with examples of poor, borderline, solid and outstanding responses. Interviewers take detailed notes, so specific actions and results matter.

### Answering hypotheticals well

1. **Clarify** the goal and the constraints: who is affected, and what does success mean?
2. **Lay out options** with their trade-offs; don't jump to one answer.
3. **Choose**, and explain why.
4. **Say how you'd measure** it and when you'd revisit it.
5. **Anchor in experience**: "When something similar happened on my team, we…".

## What "Googleyness" looks like in a story

Whatever the label, the behaviours that score well are consistent:

- **Comfort with ambiguity**: you made progress without a clear spec.
- **Collaboration**: you helped others succeed, shared credit, worked across teams.
- **Bias to action**: you tried something small instead of waiting.
- **Humility and openness**: you changed your mind when shown better data.
- **Doing the right thing for users**, even when it was harder.

Good matches from the Question Bank: [Ownership & Ambiguity](/behavioral/question-bank/ownership-and-ambiguity), [Leadership & Influence](/behavioral/question-bank/leadership-and-influence) and [Collaboration & Mentoring](/behavioral/question-bank/collaboration-and-mentoring).

## How decisions are made

*Cracking the Coding Interview* (2015) describes how a decision was made then:

- Interviewers submit written feedback to a **hiring committee** that usually doesn't include them.
- The committee looked for at least one **enthusiastic endorser** rather than uniformly average scores.

Treat the details as historical, but the lesson holds. One interviewer who writes "strong hire, here's why" is valuable, and specific stories give them the material to write it.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes at Google">

- Treating the leadership round as small talk because "it's not coding".
- Only manager-style leadership stories when you're an individual contributor. Emergent leadership (initiative, cross-team work) is what's asked for.
- Jumping to an answer in hypotheticals without clarifying or weighing options.
- Arrogance, or dismissing others' ideas. Collaboration is a core signal.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Answer this hypothetical in five steps (clarify, options, choose, measure, anchor): Your team's service has a known reliability issue, but the roadmap is full of features leadership cares about. What do you do?">

1. **Clarify**: how bad is it (incidents, user impact, error budget), and who owns the roadmap?
2. **Options**: fix it now and slip a feature; fix incrementally alongside the roadmap; or accept the risk with better mitigation, such as alerts and a runbook.
3. **Choose**: usually quantify the cost (incidents, hours, users affected) and propose a small, time-boxed fix, framed in the terms leadership cares about.
4. **Measure**: incident count and error rate, with a check-in after a set period.
5. **Anchor**: "On my last team I did this with flaky deploys: I showed the hours lost per sprint, and got two days a sprint for it."

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Google's current guide lists role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership; earlier material added "Googleyness".
- For individual contributors, leadership means emergent leadership: initiative and cross-team collaboration.
- Expect behavioral and hypothetical questions; answers are scored with rubrics.
- For hypotheticals: clarify, give options with trade-offs, choose, measure, and anchor in experience.

</Takeaways>
