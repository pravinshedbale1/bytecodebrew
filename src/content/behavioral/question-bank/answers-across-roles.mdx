Most sample answers in this course come from a backend engineer. The shape of a strong answer doesn't change with your role, but the **evidence** does. A frontend engineer's numbers are load times and conversion; a data engineer's are freshness and correctness; an SRE's are error budgets and recovery time. Here are five strong answers from other roles, one per theme, with notes on what makes each work.

## What counts as impact in each role

| Role | Typical measurable results |
| --- | --- |
| **Backend** | Latency (p50/p99), error rate, throughput, cost, incidents |
| **Frontend / web** | Load time (LCP, INP), bundle size, conversion, accessibility issues fixed, crash-free sessions |
| **Mobile** | Crash-free users, app start time, app size, store rating, release frequency |
| **Data engineering** | Pipeline freshness, job runtime, data-quality failures, cost per run, consumers served |
| **ML** | Offline metrics and, more importantly, the online business metric; inference latency and cost |
| **SRE / platform** | Availability vs SLO, mean time to detect and to recover, toil hours removed, deploy frequency |
| **QA / SDET** | Escaped defects, test run time, flakiness, coverage of critical paths |

## Frontend: impact

> **Question:** Tell me about your most impactful work.

"I cut our product page's load time roughly in half, which measurably lifted conversion.

"I was one of three frontend engineers on a retail site. Product pages took about 4.5 seconds to show the main image on mid-range Android phones, which were most of our traffic. The product team had a growing pile of 'slow page' complaints.

"First, I measured on real devices rather than my laptop: field data from our analytics, plus profiling on a low-end phone. Three things dominated. A 700 KB JavaScript bundle blocked rendering, the hero image was full-resolution, and a reviews widget loaded synchronously. Second, I split the bundle so the product page only loaded what it used. I served images in modern formats at the right size per device, and lazy-loaded the reviews below the fold. Third, I added a performance budget to CI that fails the build if the page bundle grows past a limit, so the gains would last.

"Time to the main image went from about 4.5 to 2.2 seconds on those devices. In the A/B test the faster page converted about 6% better. The budget has blocked two regressions since. I learned to measure on the devices our users actually have."

*Why it works: field data on real devices, three clear actions, a business metric (conversion) and a guard against regression.*

## Mobile: failure

> **Question:** Tell me about a mistake you made.

"I shipped an app release that crashed on launch for about 8% of Android users. I caused it.

"I'd upgraded an analytics library as part of a routine dependency update. It worked on the devices I tested, but crashed at startup on one older Android version when a particular permission wasn't granted. We release to the store in stages, but I'd skipped the staged rollout because the change 'was only a library bump'.

"Within an hour our crash dashboard spiked. I halted the rollout, reverted the dependency and shipped a hotfix that evening. Crash-free users were back above 99.5% the next day. In the post-mortem I owned two things. I skipped the staged rollout, and we had no test coverage on older OS versions.

"I changed both. Every release, including 'small' ones, now goes out in stages, starting at 5%, with an automatic halt if crash-free users drop below a threshold. And I added the three oldest OS versions we support to our device-farm test run. The automatic halt has since caught one bad release from another team at 5%, before most users saw it."

*Why it works: clear ownership, a fast response, an honest root cause ("I skipped the staged rollout") and a systemic fix with evidence.*

## Data engineering: ownership

> **Question:** Tell me about a problem nobody owned that you fixed.

"I took ownership of our daily revenue numbers being wrong a few times a month, which no single team owned.

"Finance's dashboard occasionally showed revenue 5–10% off, and they'd notice days later. The data came through four pipelines owned by three teams, so each team checked its own part and found nothing wrong.

"I offered to own it end to end for a month. First, I traced one bad day through every stage and found duplicate events. A retry in an upstream job re-emitted a batch, and our deduplication keyed on the wrong field. Second, I fixed the dedup key in our pipeline and agreed with the upstream team that they'd add a batch ID, so retries were safe on both sides. Third, I added data-quality checks after each stage: row counts against the source, no duplicate order IDs, and revenue within expected bounds. Each check alerts the owning team, so ownership is clear the next time.

"In the following quarter the dashboard was wrong zero times, and two other data issues were caught by the checks within an hour, not days. Finance stopped reconciling by hand. I learned that problems spanning several teams need someone to trace one example end to end first."

*Why it works: a cross-team problem nobody owned, a methodical root cause, a fix on both sides, and checks that made ownership permanent.*

## SRE: conflict

> **Question:** Tell me about a time you disagreed with another team.

"I disagreed with a product team that wanted to launch despite having used up their error budget. We found a middle path.

"Their checkout service had burned through its monthly error budget, and our team's policy was to freeze feature launches until reliability work was done. They had a marketing launch planned that week and pushed hard to proceed.

"First, I met their lead to understand the stakes. The launch was tied to a partner campaign that couldn't move. Second, I looked at what had burned the budget: one dependency timeout, causing about 80% of the errors. Third, I proposed launching on schedule behind a feature flag at 10% of traffic, while two of their engineers and I fixed the timeout handling that week. Then we'd ramp up as the error rate came down. I wrote it up so both managers could agree.

"They launched on time at 10%, the fix landed in four days, and they reached 100% within the week with the error rate back inside the target. Afterwards both teams agreed to add this 'flagged launch with a reliability plan' option to the policy. I learned that a rule works better with a safe way to make exceptions."

*Why it works: respect for their constraint, data on the cause, a creative middle path, and a lasting change to the process.*

## QA / SDET: leadership

> **Question:** Tell me about a time you influenced engineers outside your team.

"I got our backend teams to own their API tests, which cut the escaped defects we were finding.

"Our QA team wrote and ran all integration tests after development, so bugs were found late, and the developers saw testing as QA's job.

"First, I pulled six months of production bugs. Most were API contract issues that a test written alongside the code would have caught. Second, I built a small test template and paired with one backend team for two sprints, so their developers wrote tests in the same pull request as the code. Third, I shared that team's before-and-after defect numbers at the engineering all-hands, and offered the same pairing to any team.

"Four teams adopted it within a quarter. Escaped API defects for those teams fell by about half, and our QA team moved to exploratory and performance testing. I learned that people adopt a practice faster when they see it working for a team like theirs."

*Why it works: influence without authority, through data, a working example and a visible result.*

The point is to connect your technical result to what someone else cares about:

- the user ("pages appear twice as fast");
- the business ("6% more conversions");
- the team ("no more hand reconciliation").

Most strong answers include **both** a technical number and a user or business consequence. If you only have the technical one, ask yourself: "so what, for whom?"
