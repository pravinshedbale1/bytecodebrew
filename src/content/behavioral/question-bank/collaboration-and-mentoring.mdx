Most engineering work happens with other people, so interviewers ask how you work with them. They want to know how you treat a struggling teammate, how you collaborate across teams with different priorities, and whether you help others grow. These questions test **empathy, helpfulness and team-first behaviour**. Companies write these qualities into their values: Stripe's "Collaborate egolessly", Atlassian's "Play, as a team" and Microsoft's "One Microsoft".

## The question in its many forms

- "Tell me about a time a teammate wasn't pulling their weight."
- "Describe working with someone whose style was very different from yours."
- "Tell me about a time you worked with another team to deliver something."
- "Tell me about someone you mentored."
- "How do you onboard a new team member?"
- "Tell me about a time you helped a colleague succeed."

## What they're scoring

| Signal | Strong evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Empathy** | You tried to understand *why* before judging |
| **Helpfulness** | You invested your own time in someone else's success |
| **Directness** | You raised problems with the person first, kindly |
| **Team over self** | You cared about the team outcome, not your own credit |
| **Mentoring** | People you helped became measurably more capable |
| **Cross-team skill** | You aligned on goals, interfaces and timelines with others |

## A model answer from the book

*Cracking the Coding Interview* uses this exact theme for its S.A.R. example. A teammate on a four-person project contributed little. The candidate:

- talked to him privately with open questions, and found he was **afraid his code wasn't good enough** (not lazy);
- shared their own mistakes, so he felt safe to try;
- broke the work into a detailed spec together, so it felt manageable.

He went on to deliver his parts on time. The book then points out what the story shows: initiative, empathy, compassion, humility and teamwork, **without the candidate ever naming those qualities**. Your stories should work the same way: the actions show the trait.

## Weak vs strong (mentoring)

> **Question:** Tell me about a time you helped someone grow.

**Weak:** "I mentor juniors all the time. I'm always available for questions and I review their code carefully. They tell me I'm a good mentor."

*Notes: "Generic, no specific person or result."*

**Strong:**

"I mentored a new graduate who joined our payments team, and helped her go from first ticket to owning a service in about six months.

"In her first month she was very quiet in reviews and took days on small tickets, because she was afraid of breaking production.

"I did three things. First, I set up a weekly half-hour one-to-one and asked her what felt hardest. It was our deployment and rollback process, which nobody had explained. Second, I paired with her on two deployments, then had her lead the third while I watched. I also wrote down the steps; that became our team's deployment guide. Third, I deliberately gave her a small but real piece to own: the refund-status endpoint. I reviewed her design doc in detail rather than just the code.

"Within three months she was reviewing other people's code and asking good questions in design reviews. By six months she owned the refunds service, and she was on the on-call rotation. Her manager cited the ownership in her review. I learned that the blocker for new engineers is often confidence and missing context, not skill."

## Cross-team collaboration

For "worked with another team", show that you:

- understood **their priorities**, not just yours;
- agreed the **interface and timeline in writing**;
- created **regular, light check-ins**;
- handled the inevitable slip **without blame**.

For example: "Their quarter was already full, so I offered to write the client library change myself if they reviewed it."

## Follow-ups to expect

- "What did you do when it didn't improve?"
- "Did you involve your manager? When?"
- "What did you learn about yourself from mentoring?"
- "How do you balance helping others with your own deadlines?"

<Callout type="warning" title="Red flags">

- Describing a colleague as lazy or incompetent with no attempt to understand them.
- Escalating to the manager as the first step.
- "Mentoring" that's just answering questions, with no outcome.
- Taking credit for the mentee's achievements.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Pick one colleague you helped, or one cross-team project. Write the result in terms of what changed for THEM, or for both teams. Can you put a number or a concrete milestone on it?">

Good results for this theme:

- "she owned the service in six months";
- "he passed the on-call shadowing in four weeks";
- "the guide became the onboarding doc for 12 new hires";
- "both teams shipped in the same release with no contract breaks".

If you can't name a change in the other person or team, the story is about you being available, not about you making a difference. Pick a different one.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- These questions test empathy, helpfulness, directness and putting the team first.
- With a struggling teammate, understand why first, talk privately, then make the work manageable.
- Let your actions show the traits; don't announce them.
- Measure mentoring by what changed for the other person.

</Takeaways>
