Companies hire people who will get better. Feedback and learning questions test whether you can **hear criticism without getting defensive, change because of it, give honest feedback to others, and learn new things quickly**. Microsoft has made "growth mindset" a named hiring criterion. Netflix's culture centres on candour: giving and receiving feedback openly and admitting mistakes. Meta's own interview advice lists giving and receiving feedback as a common theme.

## The question in its many forms

- "Tell me about a time you received critical feedback."
- "What's the most useful feedback you've ever been given?"
- "Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to someone."
- "Tell me about something you learned recently."
- "Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly."
- "How do you keep your skills up to date?"

## What they're scoring

| Signal | Strong evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Openness** | You took the feedback seriously rather than explaining it away |
| **Change** | You did something concrete differently afterwards, and it stuck |
| **Candour** | You gave feedback directly, kindly and early |
| **Learning speed** | You became effective in something new quickly, and say how |
| **Self-awareness** | You know your growth areas without being told |

## Receiving feedback: the shape

1. **The feedback**: what was said, honestly, even if unflattering.
2. **Your first reaction**: admitting it stung is fine, and humanising.
3. **What you did to understand it**: asked for examples, or looked at your own work.
4. **What you changed**: a concrete habit or practice.
5. **Evidence it worked**: later feedback, a result.

**Strong sample:**

"In my first performance review as a mid-level engineer, my manager said my design docs were hard to follow: reviewers had to ask the same questions in every review. Honestly, I was a bit defensive at first, because I thought the designs themselves were good.

"I asked her for two examples, and re-read them. She was right. I jumped into the solution without stating the problem, the constraints or the alternatives. I changed three things. I started every doc with a one-paragraph problem statement and a list of non-goals. I added an 'options considered' table. And I asked one teammate to read each doc before it went out, and tell me the first question they had.

"Over the next quarter, my review meetings got noticeably shorter: two of my docs were approved with no follow-up meeting at all. At my next review she mentioned my docs as an example for the team. Now I use the same template when I review other people's designs."

## Giving feedback

Show that you:

- gave it **privately and soon**, about specific behaviour rather than personality;
- described the **impact** ("the missing tests meant the rollback took an hour") rather than a judgment;
- made it a conversation, asking for their view;
- **followed up**.

A common framework is **situation, behaviour, impact**: "In yesterday's review (situation), you approved without running it (behaviour), and it broke staging for the team (impact)."

## Learning something new quickly

Describe **how** you learned, not just that you did:

- what you read first;
- the small project you built;
- who you asked;
- how you checked your understanding;
- how soon you delivered something real with it.

"I had two weeks to become useful in Kotlin and Android. I rewrote one small screen from our app as a throwaway project. I read the team's three most-reviewed pull requests to learn their conventions. And I asked for my first real ticket to be one with heavy review. I shipped a feature in week three."

## Follow-ups to expect

- "Did you agree with the feedback?"
- "What feedback have you received that you disagreed with?"
- "How did the other person react to your feedback?"
- "What are you working on improving right now?"

<Callout type="warning" title="Red flags">

- Feedback that's secretly praise ("they said I care too much").
- Explaining why the feedback was wrong, with no change.
- Avoiding giving feedback, or only escalating to managers.
- "I learn fast", with no description of how.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Recall a piece of feedback that genuinely stung. Write what you changed afterwards and one piece of evidence the change worked. If you cannot name a change, is this the right story?">

The core of this story is the change, and the evidence for it. Good evidence:

- a later review mentioning the improvement;
- a metric (fewer review rounds, faster approvals, fewer bugs);
- someone else adopting your new practice;
- you now teaching it to others.

If nothing changed, either the feedback didn't matter much, or you haven't acted on it yet. Choose a story where you did.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- These questions test openness, real change, candour and how fast you learn.
- For feedback received: what was said, your reaction, how you understood it, what you changed, and the evidence.
- For feedback given: private, timely and specific, focused on the impact, with a follow-up.
- For learning: describe the method, not just the claim.

</Takeaways>
