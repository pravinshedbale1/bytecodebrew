Good engineers fail behavioral rounds for a handful of avoidable reasons. None is about lacking experience. They're habits in **how** stories are told, and interviewers are trained to notice them. Read this list before every interview loop.

## The red flags

| Red flag | What the interviewer concludes | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blaming others** (manager, QA, "the business") | Won't own problems; hard to work with | Describe your part, and what you'd do differently |
| **"We" everywhere** | Unclear contribution; maybe a passenger | "I" for your actions; "we" only for context |
| **Rambling** (5+ minutes, lots of context) | Unclear thinker; poor communicator | A nugget first; STAR in about 2 minutes; offer detail |
| **No result**, or no numbers | Didn't care about the outcome, or there wasn't one | Always close with what changed, with a number |
| **Fake weakness or failure** ("I work too hard") | Not self-aware, or not honest | A real one, plus what you changed |
| **Arrogance** ("obviously", "they didn't get it") | Poor collaborator | *Cracking the Coding Interview*: be specific, not arrogant. State facts, let them conclude |
| **Negativity about employers** | Will speak about us this way too | Speak toward what you want, not away from what you dislike |
| **Hypothetical answers** to "tell me about a time" | No real experience, or avoiding the question | Give a real, even adjacent, example |
| **Rehearsed scripts** | Can't handle follow-ups; may not be real | Prepare stories as notes; practise in different lengths |
| **Same story to every interviewer** | Narrow experience | 8–10 stories; two for the main themes |
| **Inflated scope** that collapses under questions | Dishonest; trust gone for the whole loop | Claim exactly your part |
| **No questions at the end** | Low interest | Prepare five; ask two or three |
| **Confidential details** (customer names, unreleased plans) | Poor judgment about information | Describe things generically |

## "Be specific, not arrogant"

The book offers a neat way to sound impressive without bragging: **be specific**. Rather than "I did all the hard parts", describe the specific hard parts you did, and let the interviewer draw the conclusion. Specific facts are both more credible and more modest.

| Arrogant | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| "I'm the best engineer on my team." | "I'm the person the team pages for payment incidents; I handled 14 last quarter." |
| "I basically built the whole system." | "I designed the retry layer and wrote about two-thirds of the service; two others built the reporting." |
| "They didn't understand the problem." | "They hadn't seen the reporting queries yet, so I ran them on both databases and shared the numbers." |

## "Limit details"

The book's other advice: technical over-explanation loses non-specialist interviewers. Give the key points, translate them into impact, and **offer** depth. Its example: state that you reduced search from O(n) to O(log n) in 90% of cases with a specific algorithm, then add, "I can go into more details if you'd like."

## Behaviour in the room counts too

- **Listen to the question** and answer that question. If unsure, ask: "Do you mean a conflict with a peer or with a manager?"
- **Take a moment to think**: "Let me think of the best example" is fine.
- **Watch the interviewer.** If they look lost or start writing less, summarise and offer to move on.
- **Be warm and honest.** For leads especially, the book notes that interviewers watch how you interact. Too arrogant or too passive both count against you.

<Practice task="Record yourself answering two behavioral questions, then check the transcript against the red-flag table. Count the uses of we versus I, measure the length, and mark every sentence that blames someone.">

Typical first-time findings:

- "we" outnumbers "I" two to one;
- the situation takes 40% of the time;
- there's no number in the result;
- one sentence quietly blames another team.

Fix one habit at a time and record again. Two or three rounds usually make the improvement obvious. Listening to yourself is uncomfortable, and it's the fastest way to improve.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Most behavioral failures are habits: blame, "we", rambling, no result, fake weaknesses, arrogance, negativity.
- Be specific, not arrogant: state facts and let the interviewer conclude.
- Limit detail and offer depth; answer the question asked; ask a clarifying question if needed.
- Record and review your answers; fix one habit at a time.

</Takeaways>
