The interviews are over and an offer arrives, or two. This is the step people prepare for least, even though a single conversation can change your compensation more than a year of raises. *Cracking the Coding Interview* sums up its advice in two words: **negotiate**. This lesson covers how to compare offers properly and how to negotiate without fear.

## After the loop

- **Follow up politely** if you haven't heard back in the timeframe the recruiter gave. Silence usually means a busy recruiter or a slow process, not a rejection.
- **If rejected**, ask whether there's feedback (many big companies won't give it, but it doesn't hurt). Ask when you can re-apply. A rejection is often about the match on that day, not a permanent verdict.
- **Send thank-you notes** if you like. Amazon's guide calls them optional, and suggests one or two days later through your recruiter, mentioning something specific you discussed.

## Compare offers on the whole package

The book warns that the biggest mistake is **looking only at the salary**. Compare:

| Component | How to compare |
| --- | --- |
| **Base salary** | The guaranteed part |
| **Annual bonus** | Target percentage and how reliably it's paid; ask current employees |
| **Equity** (stock grants, RSUs, options) | Annualise it over the vesting period; note the vesting schedule and the company's stage |
| **Joining bonus, relocation** | Spread these one-time amounts over the years you expect to stay |
| **Cost of living and taxes** | Cities differ a lot; compare take-home pay, not headline numbers |
| **Benefits** | Health insurance, retirement contributions, leave, learning budget |

And beyond money, the book's checklist:

- **Career growth**: How much will I learn? How do engineers get promoted here? Is the team growing? Does the name help my next move?
- **Stability**: weigh it against growth, depending on your situation (visa, savings, appetite for risk).
- **Happiness**: the manager and teammates (often the biggest factor), the culture, typical hours, and the product.
- **Location**: are there other good employers nearby if this doesn't work out?

## How to negotiate

From the book's negotiation advice:

1. **Just do it.** It feels uncomfortable, but recruiters don't withdraw offers because a candidate negotiated respectfully, especially at larger companies.
2. **Have a viable alternative.** Another offer, or a strong current job, is what makes your request real.
3. **Make a specific ask**: a specific number, not "a bit more".
4. **Ask a little above your target**: negotiations usually meet somewhere in the middle.
5. **Think beyond salary**: equity, joining bonus, relocation in cash, start date, level. Companies often have more room on these than on base salary.
6. **Use the medium you're best in**: phone is common, but email is fine if it helps you be clear and calm.

The book also notes that big companies pay within **bands per level**. You can negotiate within your band, but a big jump usually means arguing for a **higher level**, based on your experience and how the interviews went.

A simple, honest script:

> "Thank you, I'm really excited about this team. I have another offer at [specific total]. Joining you is my first preference. If you can bring the total to [specific number], ideally through the joining bonus or equity, I'm ready to accept."

Only say it if it's true. Never invent offers: it can be checked, and it destroys trust.

## Before you accept

- Get the offer **in writing**, with the level, the start date and every component.
- Understand the **vesting schedule** and any clawback conditions on bonuses.
- Ask about the **notice period** and joining date, and plan your resignation professionally.
- Leave your current job well: hand over properly and stay on good terms. The book stresses that your network is your most valuable career asset.

<Callout type="tip" title="After you join">

The book's career advice for the long term:

- Set a career timeline and review it every year.
- Build strong relationships, and keep in touch with people who leave.
- Ask your manager clearly for the work you want.
- Interview occasionally, even when you're happy, to keep your skills and market knowledge fresh.

</Callout>

<Practice task="You have two offers: A pays a higher base salary; B pays less base but a larger stock grant vesting over four years plus a joining bonus, at a faster-growing company. List the questions you would ask before deciding, and what you would negotiate with your preferred company.">

Questions to ask:

- What is B's equity actually worth: public stock price, or a private valuation (riskier)?
- What is the vesting schedule, and is there a cliff?
- How reliable are the bonuses at each company?
- What are the teams, managers and growth paths like?
- What's the take-home pay after tax and cost of living?

Annualise everything over about three to four years before comparing. Then negotiate with your **preferred** company, citing the other offer honestly, with a specific ask, often on the component they can flex most (joining bonus or equity).

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Compare whole packages: base, bonus, equity, one-time payments, cost of living and benefits, annualised.
- Weigh growth, stability, team and location, not just money.
- Negotiate: have an alternative, make a specific ask, ask a little above target, think beyond salary.
- Big companies pay in bands per level; a large jump means arguing for a higher level.
- Get everything in writing, and leave your current job on good terms.

</Takeaways>
