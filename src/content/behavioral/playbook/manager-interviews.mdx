Engineering-manager loops look like engineering loops with more behavioral rounds, but the bar is different. As a manager, **your results are your team's results**, so every story must show how you got outcomes **through people**: hiring them, growing them, aligning them, and sometimes letting them go. *Cracking the Coding Interview* lists what interviewers examine in leads and managers:

- teamwork and leadership;
- prioritisation;
- communication at every level;
- "getting things done": balancing planning against execution, structuring projects and motivating people.

## What manager rounds cover

| Area | What they're testing | Typical question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **People development** | You grow people, and they get promoted | "Tell me about someone you developed into a bigger role." |
| **Performance** | You address underperformance early, fairly and decisively | "Tell me about an underperformer you managed." |
| **Hiring** | You hire well and raise the bar | "Tell me about a hire you made and how you assessed them." |
| **Execution** | Your team delivers predictably | "Tell me about a project at risk that you got back on track." |
| **Team health** | Morale, burnout, conflict, inclusion | "Tell me about a time your team was burning out." |
| **Managing up and across** | You represent your team and push back on leadership | "Tell me about a time you pushed back on your own leadership." |
| **Technical judgment** | You can still evaluate designs and trade-offs | "How do you balance tech debt and features?" |
| **Change** | You lead through reorganisations and new directions | "Tell me about leading your team through a big change." |

## Sample answers

### Performance: an underperformer

> "I took over a team where one senior engineer, I'll call him R, was missing most commitments and his code needed several rounds of review. The previous manager had given only vague feedback.
>
> "First, I made the expectations concrete. In our one-to-one I showed three specific examples, compared them with what I expected at his level, and asked for his view. It came out that he'd been moved onto a stack he didn't know and felt embarrassed to ask for help. Second, we agreed a six-week plan: pairing with our strongest engineer on that stack for two weeks, then owning one well-defined component, with weekly check-ins against written goals. Third, I documented the plan and the check-ins and kept HR informed, so the process would be fair whichever way it went.
>
> "By week six he'd delivered the component on time, with normal review cycles. Six months later he was mentoring others on that stack. Not every case turns around. I've also had to manage someone out after a similar plan, and the clear written expectations made that fair and unsurprising for him. What I learned is that underperformance is often missing context or confidence, and that clear expectations help in both outcomes."

**Why it works:** specific expectations, curiosity about the cause, a fair written process, a measurable outcome, and maturity about the cases that don't turn around.

### Team health: burnout

> "After two back-to-back launches my team's on-call load and overtime were clearly unsustainable: two people mentioned being exhausted in one-to-ones, and pages had tripled. I did three things. I declared a two-week stabilisation period and negotiated it with my director using the page count and the incident trend. I asked the team to rank the top sources of pages, and we fixed the top five, which removed about 60% of the pages. And I changed our planning so every sprint reserved 20% for operational work. Pages stayed at the lower level for the next two quarters, and in our next engagement survey the team's workload score improved the most of any team in the department."

### Hiring: raising the bar

> "I needed two senior engineers quickly, and the hiring manager before me had used one generic interview loop. I rewrote it around what the role actually needed: a design round on a problem from our domain, and a behavioral round on cross-team influence. I trained two of my engineers as interviewers and calibrated them on two mock candidates. We hired two people in ten weeks. Both were rated top performers at their first review, and one now leads our platform work. I also learned to say no: I turned down one strong coder who dismissed every question about collaboration, even though we were under pressure."

## Pitfalls specific to manager interviews

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">

- **Only "I" stories with no team**: for managers, credit should go to the team, and your part is how you enabled them.
- **Only people stories** with no delivery results, or **only delivery** with no people stories. You need both.
- **Never having managed anyone out** (if you've managed for a while), or describing it without care.
- **Blaming previous managers or leadership.**
- **Not being able to evaluate a design**: many companies still test technical depth for managers. The book notes Google in particular holds managers to a high coding bar.

</Callout>

Every question in the bank's [engineer-manager theme](/behavioral/question-bank/all-questions) is also in the [Answer Drill](/behavioral/question-bank/behavioral-drill). Choose "Engineering manager" as the theme.

<Practice task="Prepare one story for each of the eight areas in the table. For each, write the team outcome AND your specific contribution. Which areas have no story, and are they deal-breakers for the roles you are targeting?">

Most new managers lack stories for **hiring**, **managing out** and **pushing back on leadership**. If you can't get real experience before your interviews, use the closest adjacent experience and say so. For example:

- "I haven't owned a hiring decision yet, but I've run 30 interviews and redesigned our loop."
- "I haven't managed someone out, but here's how I handled a serious performance issue as a tech lead."

Honest, adjacent stories beat invented ones, and interviewers can tell the difference.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Manager interviews judge results achieved through people: development, performance, hiring, execution, team health, managing up and change.
- Each story needs a team outcome plus your specific enabling actions.
- Show fairness and care in hard people decisions, and evidence that you still have technical judgment.
- Prepare one story per area; use honest adjacent experience where you lack direct experience.

</Takeaways>
