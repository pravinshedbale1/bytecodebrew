In India, long notice periods are common: two or three months is typical at many companies, written into the employment contract. That shapes the whole job switch. Recruiters ask about it early, some prefer candidates who can join sooner, and the gap between accepting an offer and joining leaves room for **counter-offers** and second thoughts on both sides. Handling this period professionally protects your offer, your reputation and your references.

<Callout type="note" title="Check your own contract">

Notice-period terms, buy-out rules and relieving procedures vary by employer and contract. What follows is common practice, not legal advice. Your offer letter, your current contract and HR are the authorities.

</Callout>

## Talking about notice period in interviews

- **Be accurate.** State your contractual notice period and whether your employer allows early release or a buy-out. Misstating it to look attractive backfires when the joining date slips.
- **Say what's possible**: "My notice is 90 days. My current employer has allowed early release after a proper handover before, so 60 days is realistic. I'd confirm once I resign."
- **If you're already serving notice**, say so, with the last working day.
- **Don't let it dominate.** Answer briefly and steer back to the role.

## Buy-outs and early release

Some employers let you leave early if you or the new employer **pay for the remaining notice period** (a "buy-out"), or simply release you early after a good handover. If the new company wants you sooner:

- ask whether they'll **reimburse the buy-out**, which is often negotiable as part of the joining bonus;
- get any such promise **in writing** in the offer;
- never leave without a proper release. **Relieving and experience letters** are routinely checked in background verification at your next employer.

## Counter-offers

When you resign, your current employer may offer more money or a promotion to keep you. Think it through **before** you resign:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Why did I start looking? | If the reasons were scope, growth, team or manager, money alone doesn't fix them |
| Will the counter-offer change the work? | A raise without new responsibilities often leaves the original frustration in place |
| How will I be seen after accepting? | Some managers see a counter-offer as a temporary fix |
| Have I committed to the new company? | Accepting an offer and then withdrawing harms your reputation with them, and recruiters talk |

There's no universal right answer. Sometimes a counter-offer genuinely addresses what you wanted. But decide on **your reasons**, not on the size of the new number, and decide quickly.

<Callout type="warning" title="Offer shopping">

Using one offer only to extract a raise elsewhere, accepting several offers and joining one, or withdrawing after accepting may feel normal in a hot market. It burns bridges with recruiters and hiring managers you may meet again. Negotiate openly before accepting; once you accept, honour it unless something serious changes, and if it does, tell them promptly and directly.

</Callout>

## Resigning well

1. **Tell your manager first**, in person or on a call, before telling the team. Keep it short and positive: what you're grateful for, and that you've accepted another role.
2. **Put it in writing** the same day, with your intended last working day.
3. **Plan the handover**: a written handover document, knowledge-transfer sessions, and named owners for everything you own.
4. **Keep working properly until the last day.** People remember how you leave.
5. **Collect what you need**: relieving and experience letters, final settlement details, and your provident fund (PF) transfer information.
6. **Stay in touch.** *Cracking the Coding Interview* stresses that your network is your most valuable career asset; former colleagues are your future referrals.

## Between accepting and joining

- **Keep in touch** with the recruiter and hiring manager; a short update every few weeks is enough.
- **Respond quickly** to background-verification requests.
- **Prepare to start well**: read about the company's domain and technology, and think about your first 90 days. See the book's advice on setting a career timeline in [Offers & Negotiation](/behavioral/playbook/offers-and-negotiation).

<Practice task="Write the two-sentence answer you would give if asked about your notice period in an interview, and the three questions you would ask yourself if your current employer made a counter-offer.">

A good notice-period answer is accurate and concrete: "My notice period is 60 days. I can likely reduce it with a handover, and I'd confirm the exact date as soon as I resign."

The counter-offer questions:

1. Does it fix the reason I started looking?
2. Will the work actually change, or only the pay?
3. Am I comfortable with how I'll be seen if I stay?

If the honest answers are no, no and no, the counter-offer is a delay, not a solution.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Long notice periods are common in India; state yours accurately and say what's realistically possible.
- Buy-outs and early release are negotiable; get promises in writing and never skip a proper relieving process.
- Judge counter-offers on whether they fix your reasons for leaving, not on the number.
- Resign to your manager first, hand over properly, collect your letters, and keep the relationship.

</Takeaways>
