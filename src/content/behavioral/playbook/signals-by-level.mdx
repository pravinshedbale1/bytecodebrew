The same question, "Tell me about a project you led", should get a very different answer from a new graduate than from a staff engineer. Behavioral interviews assess **whether** you show the signals and also **at what scope**, and that scope often decides the level you're offered. Under-pitching your stories is one of the most common reasons experienced engineers get down-levelled.

## What changes as you go up

| Dimension | Junior | Mid-level | Senior | Staff / principal | Engineering manager |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Scope** | A task or feature | A project or component | A system, or a multi-person project | Several teams or an organisation | A team, and its output |
| **Ambiguity** | Clear tasks | Defined problem, open solution | Vague problem, you define the solution | You find the problem | You set the team's direction |
| **Influence** | Your own work | Your team | Your team and neighbouring teams | Across the organisation; leadership | Your team, peers and leadership |
| **Time horizon** | Days–weeks | Weeks–a quarter | A quarter to a year | One to several years | Quarters to years |
| **People** | Learns from others | Helps peers | Mentors; raises the bar | Grows senior engineers; shapes culture | Hires, develops, manages performance |
| **Typical story** | "I fixed a hard bug and learned X." | "I owned the feature end to end." | "I led the migration and aligned three teams." | "I identified a cross-org problem and changed how we work." | "I turned around a struggling team." |

## Experienced candidates: what the book warns

*Cracking the Coding Interview* has three warnings for experienced candidates:

- **Experience raises expectations.** Answers to questions like "What was the hardest bug you've faced?" are expected to be **more in-depth and impressive**.
- **Leads are judged by how they interact, not only by their stories.** Interviewers watch you in the room. Coming across as **too arrogant or too passive** can count against you as a lead.
- **Leads and managers are assessed on four things:** teamwork and leadership, prioritisation, communication at every level, and **getting things done**. That means balancing planning against delivery, structuring projects, and motivating people.

## Pitching at the right level

- **Choose stories at your target level.** For a senior role, at least half your stories should involve leading others, cross-team work, or defining the problem.
- **Make the scope explicit**: "four engineers, two teams, about a year", "the platform used by 30 services".
- **Show the decisions, not just the work**: what you chose not to do, the trade-offs, the risks you managed.
- **Show multiplying effects**: what other people could do because of you (tools, docs, mentoring, standards).
- **Don't inflate.** If you led a part, say so; follow-up questions will reveal the real scope anyway.

## Manager interviews

Engineering-manager loops add people-specific themes. Prepare stories for each:

- **Hiring**: a hire you made and how you assessed them; a hire that didn't work out.
- **Performance**: helping an underperformer improve, or managing them out fairly.
- **Growth**: someone you developed into a bigger role.
- **Team health**: burnout, conflict between team members, low morale.
- **Delivery**: a project at risk that you got back on track.
- **Upward management**: pushing back on leadership; representing the team's needs.
- **Change**: a reorganisation, new process or direction you led the team through.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common levelling mistakes">

- Experienced engineers telling only "I implemented X" stories: that's the bar for a mid-level engineer.
- Leaving out scope numbers (team size, systems affected, duration).
- Senior candidates with no story about growing other people.
- Managers telling only technical stories, or only people stories.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Write the scope line (people, teams, systems, duration, impact) for your three best stories. Which level does each one signal? If you are targeting senior, how many of your stories involve leading others or cross-team work?">

A scope line reads like: "I led three engineers over two quarters, migrating the payment service used by six teams. It cut incidents by 70%." For a **senior** target, aim for at least half your stories to include:

- leading others, even informally;
- cross-team alignment;
- defining an ambiguous problem.

If you're short, look for informal leadership you haven't counted: running an incident, leading a design review, onboarding new people, or owning a migration plan.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Behavioral rounds assess signals and scope, and scope drives levelling.
- From junior to staff, scope, ambiguity, influence, time horizon and people impact all grow.
- Experienced candidates face higher expectations, and leads are judged on prioritisation, communication and getting things done.
- Make scope explicit with numbers, show decisions and multiplying effects, and never inflate.

</Takeaways>
