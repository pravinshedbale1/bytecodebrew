There are hundreds of behavioral questions, and nobody can prepare an answer for each. You don't need to. The questions come from about **eight themes**, and one good story usually answers two or three of them. So the whole preparation reduces to this: find **eight to ten real stories**, write each one once in STAR form, and know which themes each can answer. This lesson walks you through mining your experience for stories and gives you a story bank to fill in, right on this page.

## Step 1: mine your experience

*Cracking the Coding Interview* suggests an **interview preparation grid**. Put every project, job or major activity from your resume along the top, the common question types down the side, and fill in a few keywords where they meet:

| Question type ↓ / Project → | Payments idempotency | Search re-index | Mentoring intern | Monolith split |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Challenges | Duplicate charges under retries | 40M docs, zero downtime | — | Shared DB tables |
| Mistakes / failures | — | Missed a mapping change, 2 h outage | — | Under-estimated by 2 months |
| Enjoyed | Root-cause hunt | — | Watching her ship | — |
| Leadership | Design doc, 2 teams | — | Weekly 1:1s | Drove the plan |
| Conflicts | Pushback on shared client change | — | — | Disagreed with tech lead on order |
| What you'd do differently | Add the alert first | Dry run on a copy | — | Strangler pattern earlier |

Reading down each column shows you which projects are rich (the monolith split appears everywhere) and which themes are thin (here, nothing about enjoying work on the migration).

Good places to dig for stories:

- **Incidents and bugs** you chased: ownership, pressure, technical depth.
- **Design reviews and code reviews** where you disagreed: conflict, influence, judgment.
- **Projects that slipped** or failed: failure, trade-offs, learning.
- **Things nobody asked you to do** (tooling, docs, alerts, onboarding guides): ownership.
- **People you helped**: interns, new joiners, other teams.
- **Feedback** that stung: growth.
- **Your proudest launch**: impact, customers.

## Step 2: pick the 8–10 best stories

A story makes the cut if it is:

- **Real and yours**: you played a central role, and you remember the details.
- **Recent**: ideally the last two or three years. Older or college stories are fine early in a career.
- **Specific**: one situation, not "in general I always…".
- **Tense**: something was hard, uncertain, or contested.
- **At your level**: the scope matches the job you want (see [Signals by Level](/behavioral/playbook/signals-by-level)).
- **Measurable**: there's a result you can put a number on.

Make sure the set includes **at least one real failure** and **at least one conflict**. They're the most commonly asked themes, and the ones people most often lack.

## Step 3: write each story once, in STAR

For each story, write short notes, not a script: a title, the situation and task in two sentences, three or four numbered actions with reasons, a result with a number, and what you learned. Then reduce it to **keywords** you can glance at. The book suggests this, so the grid stays easy to study and recall.

## Step 4: map stories to themes

Tag each story with every theme it can answer. A strong set covers every theme at least once, and the important themes (conflict, failure, ownership, impact) twice. Then you have a spare story if one has already been used in the same loop.

## Step 5: rehearse out loud, in three lengths

Practise each story:

- in **30 seconds**: the headline and result, for "tell me about yourself" or a quick follow-up;
- in **2 minutes**: the full STAR;
- as a **deep dive**: ten minutes of follow-ups, ideally with a friend asking "why?".

Say it out loud, not in your head; the first spoken version is always too long. Record one and listen back: you'll hear every "basically" and every "we".

<Callout type="tip" title="One story, several angles">

The same incident can answer different questions if you change the **emphasis**:

- **Ownership**: "I picked it up although it wasn't my service."
- **Failure**: "The bug was in code I had reviewed and approved."
- **Pressure**: "We had four hours before the sale."
- **Conflict**: "The on-call lead wanted to roll back; I argued for a forward fix."

Lead with the part that matches the question. Don't retell the whole thing the same way every time.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">

- **Too few stories**, so the same one is told to three interviewers in one loop. Interviewers compare notes.
- **Only success stories**: you'll freeze on "tell me about a failure".
- **Stories where you were a bystander.**
- **Invented or exaggerated details**: they fall apart under follow-up questions and destroy trust.
- **Scripts memorised word for word** instead of notes.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Using the grid above, list your last two jobs or main projects as columns and fill in at least one cell per row. Which themes are empty, and where could a story for them come from?">

The usual gaps are **failure** and **conflict**, because people don't think of them as stories. Look for:

- a deadline you missed;
- a bug you shipped;
- an estimate that was badly off;
- a design or code review where someone disagreed with you (or you with them);
- a priority clash with another team.

Small is fine. A well-told disagreement in a code review beats a vague story about a "big conflict". Add each story to the bank above and tag its themes until no row is empty.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- About eight themes cover most behavioral questions; one story answers two or three of them.
- Mine your resume with a preparation grid: projects across the top, question types down the side.
- Keep 8–10 real, recent, specific stories, including at least one failure and one conflict.
- Write each once in STAR, as notes, then tag its themes until every theme is covered.
- Rehearse out loud in three lengths: 30 seconds, 2 minutes and a deep dive.

</Takeaways>
