"So, tell me about yourself" is the first question in many interviews, and it sets the interviewer's first impression of you. Most people answer it badly in one of two ways: they recite their resume line by line, or they talk about their childhood love of computers for four minutes. A good answer is a **90-second pitch**: who you are now, the path that got you here with a few pieces of evidence along the way, and why you're talking to this company today.

## The structure

*Cracking the Coding Interview* recommends a mostly chronological pitch that it says works for about 95% of candidates:

1. **Current role, headline only.** One sentence.
2. **Education** (short, and shorter the more experienced you are).
3. **Career so far.** The path, with a reason for each move.
4. **Current role in detail.** What you own, and one highlight.
5. **Outside work** (optional): only if it's technical or says something about you.
6. **Wrap-up.** Why you're looking, and why this company.

## A sample pitch

For a backend engineer with five years' experience, applying to a payments company:

> "I'm a backend engineer at Northwind Retail, where I've spent the last three years on the payments platform, mostly in Java and Spring.
>
> I studied computer science, and my first job was at a services company building APIs for banking clients. That's where I got into payments, and also where I realised I wanted to own a product, not hand projects over.
>
> So I joined Northwind Retail. I started on the order service, then moved to payments when we rebuilt checkout. The piece I'm proudest of is the retry and idempotency layer: it took duplicate charges to zero, and it handled four times the normal traffic in our festive sale. For the last year I've also been the go-to person for payment incidents, and I've mentored two new joiners on the team.
>
> Outside work I contribute to a small open-source library for retry policies, which came out of that work.
>
> I'm looking for a bigger scale of payments problem, which is why your team caught my attention: you process far more volume than we do, and you're building in-house risk systems that I'd love to work on."

That's about 90 seconds. It contains:

- **Evidence of success**, dropped in casually: proudest piece, zero duplicates, 4× traffic, go-to person, mentoring.
- **A reason for each move**, so the path makes sense.
- **A specific reason for this company.**

## Sprinkle in proof, don't list adjectives

"I'm passionate, hard-working and a fast learner" is unprovable and forgettable. Instead, mention things that **show** those qualities: a promotion, being asked to lead something, being recruited by a former manager, a launch, a number. The book calls this "sprinkle in shows of successes".

## Adjust for your situation

| Situation | Adjust the pitch |
| --- | --- |
| **Fresher / new grad** | Lead with education, internships and one strong project; end with what you want to learn |
| **Experienced (8+ years)** | Compress the early career to one sentence; spend the time on the last two roles and your scope |
| **Career switch** | Make the switch the story: what you did before, what pulled you over, what you've built since |
| **Gap in your career** | One honest sentence ("I took a year off for family / health / study"), then move on |
| **Short stints** | Give the reason once ("the startup shut down"), without apologising |

## What to leave out

- Your whole resume: they have it.
- Personal details unrelated to work (family, age, hometown), unless they're relevant.
- Anything negative about current or past employers.
- Salary or reasons that sound purely about money.

<Callout type="tip" title="Hobbies: only if they add something">

The book's advice is to mention a hobby when it's **technical** (side projects, open source, hackathons), **unusual** enough to spark conversation, or when it **shows a quality** like persistence or learning. "I like travelling and music" adds nothing. "I've been building a home-automation system with a Raspberry Pi" does.

</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">

- **Too long**: past two minutes, the interviewer stops listening.
- **Reading the resume aloud.**
- **No wrap-up**: ending in mid-career leaves them wondering why you're here.
- **Generic company reasons** ("great culture", "big brand").
- **Starting with "So, I was born in…"**

</Callout>

<Practice task="Write your own pitch in five or six sentences following the structure, then time yourself saying it. What do you cut if it runs over two minutes?">

Cut, in this order:

1. Education details beyond one sentence.
2. Early-career detail: compress to one sentence with the reason you moved.
3. Technology lists: say "Java and Spring", not eight frameworks.
4. The hobby, unless it's technical.

Keep the current-role highlight with its number, and the specific "why this company" ending. Those are what the interviewer remembers. Practise until it's 60–90 seconds and sounds conversational, not recited.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Give a 90-second pitch, not a resume reading: now, the path with reasons, now in detail, why here.
- Drop in evidence (promotions, launches, numbers, being asked to lead) instead of adjectives.
- Adapt the weighting to freshers, experienced engineers and career changes.
- Always end with why this company, and make it specific.

</Takeaways>
