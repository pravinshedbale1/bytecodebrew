"Do you have any questions for me?" comes at the end of almost every interview, and "No, I think you covered everything" is a wasted chance. *Cracking the Coding Interview* points out that the quality of your questions is a factor in the decision, consciously or not. Good questions also get you information you genuinely need to choose between offers.

## Three kinds of good question

The book groups good questions into three kinds.

**1. Genuine questions**: things you actually want to know about daily life.

- "What does a typical week look like for someone on this team?"
- "How does planning work: who decides what the team builds next?"
- "What brought you to this company, and what has been most challenging?"
- "How are on-call and incidents handled?"
- "What does success look like in the first six months in this role?"

**2. Insightful questions**: they show you've done your homework.

- "I read that you moved from a monolith to services last year. What would you do differently?"
- "Your blog mentions you use event sourcing for orders. How do you handle schema changes in old events?"
- "The job description mentions building in-house risk systems. Is that a new team?"

**3. Passion questions**: they show you want to learn and contribute.

- "I'm very interested in distributed systems. What opportunities are there to work on the platform side?"
- "I haven't used your stream processor. Could you tell me a bit about why you chose it?"

## Match the question to the interviewer

| Interviewer | Good topics |
| --- | --- |
| **Engineer / peer** | Code review culture, tech stack, tooling, on-call, what they enjoy |
| **Hiring manager** | Team goals, expectations, how performance is measured, growth paths |
| **Senior leader** | Direction, biggest challenges, how the team fits the company strategy |
| **Recruiter** | Process, timelines, levels, next steps (and compensation, at the right stage) |

## Questions to avoid

- Anything answered on the first page of their website.
- Salary, leave and benefits with an engineer interviewer (ask the recruiter).
- "Did I pass?" or "How did I do?"
- Questions that sound negative ("Is the work-life balance as bad as people say?"). Ask neutrally instead: "What are typical working hours like, including around releases?"

<Callout type="tip" title="Prepare five, ask two or three">

Some will be answered during the interview. Prepare five, and keep two in reserve in case the conversation ran long. Also, actually **listen** to the answers. A follow-up question on what they said ("You mentioned the migration. How did the team decide the order?") is the strongest question of all.

</Callout>

<Practice task="Write one genuine, one insightful and one passion question for a company you are interviewing with. Which needed research, and where did you find it?">

The **insightful** question is the one that usually needs research. Good sources:

- the company's engineering blog;
- conference talks by their engineers;
- the job description;
- recent product launches.

Genuine and passion questions can be prepared from your own priorities: what matters to you in a team, and what you want to learn next. If the answers would actually change your decision, they're good questions.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Always have questions; their quality is noticed.
- Genuine questions are about daily life, insightful ones show your research, and passion ones show what you want to learn.
- Match the topic to the interviewer, and save compensation for the recruiter.
- Prepare five; the best question is a follow-up on something they said.

</Takeaways>
