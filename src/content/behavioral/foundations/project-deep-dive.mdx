Sooner or later an interviewer says, "Tell me about a project you're proud of", and then keeps asking "why?" for twenty minutes. The project deep dive tests two things at once: whether you really did what your resume says, and how deep your technical judgment goes. It's common for experienced engineers, and it's often where the decision about your **level** is made. The defence is simple: know two or three projects inside out.

## Choose the right projects

*Cracking the Coding Interview* advises mastering **two or three projects**, chosen because:

- the project had **genuinely challenging parts**, beyond "I learned a lot";
- you played a **central role**, ideally on the challenging parts;
- you can discuss it at **technical depth**.

A smaller project where you made the key decisions beats a famous project where you fixed tickets. Pick projects that match the level you're applying for: a senior role wants a project where you shaped the design or led others, not one where you implemented someone else's plan.

## Prepare these answers for each project

For each project, prepare the answers to these questions in advance. The book lists challenges, mistakes, technical decisions, technology choices and trade-offs, and what you'd do differently; scaling questions are common follow-ups.

| Area | Be ready to explain |
| --- | --- |
| **The problem** | Who it was for, why it mattered, what happened before |
| **Your role** | What you owned, who else was involved, how you split the work |
| **Architecture** | A whiteboard sketch: components, data flow, storage |
| **Key decisions** | Two or three choices, the alternatives, and why you chose as you did |
| **Hardest part** | The specific technical challenge and how you solved it |
| **Mistakes** | What went wrong and what you changed |
| **Results** | Metrics: latency, cost, incidents, revenue, adoption |
| **Do differently** | With hindsight, what you'd change, and why |
| **Scale** | What breaks at 10× load, and how you'd fix it |

## The structure of a good deep dive

1. **One-minute overview**: the problem, the users, your role and the outcome.
2. **Architecture**: sketch it, naming only the components that matter.
3. **Let them steer**: "I can go into the data model, the rollout, or the performance work. Which is most interesting?" Interviewers like choosing, and it gets you to your strongest material.
4. **Go deep on decisions**: always give the alternatives and the trade-off.

Here's how a decision sounds when explained well:

> "We needed to fan out order events to four consumers. I chose Kafka over a simple queue because two consumers needed to replay history after a bug, and Kafka keeps the log. The trade-off was operational cost: we had to run and monitor a cluster, so I pushed for the managed service, even though it cost more per month."

## Questions you'll get, and what they're testing

| Question | Testing |
| --- | --- |
| "Why did you choose X over Y?" | Judgment and trade-off awareness |
| "What was the hardest bug?" | Depth and persistence |
| "How did you test it? How did you roll it out?" | Engineering maturity |
| "What would break at 10× traffic?" | Systems thinking |
| "What did your teammates do?" | Honesty about your own role |
| "What would you do differently?" | Self-awareness and reflection |
| "How did you measure success?" | Impact orientation |

Experienced candidates are held to a higher bar here. The book notes that an experienced engineer's answer to "what was the hardest bug you've faced?" is expected to be more in-depth and impressive.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">

- **Overclaiming**: saying you designed something you only implemented. Follow-ups expose it quickly.
- **"We decided"** with no view on what you thought or proposed.
- **No trade-offs**: every decision presented as obviously right.
- **Not knowing the numbers**: traffic, data size, latency, team size.
- **Drowning in detail**: give the overview first, then go deep where they point.
- **Confidential details**: describe the problem generically, with no internal names or secrets. Interviewers respect that.

</Callout>

## Interview questions

<QA q="How do you talk about a project where most of the work was done by others?">
Be precise about your part and generous about theirs: "The design was the tech lead's; I owned the migration tooling and the rollout." Then go deep on your part — the decisions you made, the problems you solved, and what you'd change. Interviewers are scoring your contribution, and being honest about the boundary is itself a strong signal. If your part is small, choose a different project for the deep dive.
</QA>

<Practice task="For your strongest project, write the one-minute overview and list three decisions with their alternatives. Then answer: what would break first at ten times the traffic?">

A good overview names:

- the **problem** and the users;
- your **role**;
- a **metric** for the outcome.

Each decision should read: we needed X; options were A and B; I chose A because…; the cost was…. For the 10× question, think about the usual suspects:

- a single database or hot partition;
- synchronous calls in a chain;
- caches with low hit rates;
- batch jobs that won't fit their time window;
- rate limits from third parties.

Say what you'd monitor to see it coming, and what you'd change first. This connects to the [System Design](/system-design/hld) track.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Master two or three projects where you had a central role on the challenging parts.
- Prepare the problem, your role, the architecture, key decisions with alternatives, the hardest part, mistakes, results, what you'd do differently, and scale.
- Overview first, then let the interviewer steer; go deep on decisions and trade-offs.
- Be exact about your own contribution; overclaiming is exposed by follow-ups.

</Takeaways>
