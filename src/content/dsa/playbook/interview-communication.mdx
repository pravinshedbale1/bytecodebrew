Many candidates who could solve the problem still fail the interview, and many who don't finish still pass. The difference is almost always **communication**. The interviewer can't see inside your head. All they can score is what you say and write. This lesson explains what interviewers write down about you and gives you a script for each phase of a 45-minute round, including the moments you're stuck.

## What the interviewer is scoring

Most companies use a rubric close to this one:

| Signal | What they look for | How you show it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Problem solving** | Can you break down an unfamiliar problem and find an efficient approach? | Brute force first, then name its waste and improve it |
| **Coding** | Clean, correct, idiomatic code without hand-holding | Clear names, small helpers, no half-finished lines |
| **Verification** | Do you find your own bugs? | Trace an example by hand; test edge cases unprompted |
| **Communication** | Can a teammate follow your thinking? | Narrate decisions and trade-offs, not keystrokes |
| **Complexity** | Do you understand the cost of what you wrote? | State time and space, with the reason |

An optimal solution reached in silence, with no testing, scores worse than a clear, well-tested solution that came from a hint. **Hints are normal.** What counts is how you use them.

## The 45-minute shape

| Minutes | Phase | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Clarify | Agree on inputs, outputs, constraints and edge cases |
| 5–15 | Approach | Brute force → improvement → agreement before coding |
| 15–35 | Code | Write it cleanly while narrating |
| 35–42 | Test | Trace an example, then edge cases; fix bugs calmly |
| 42–45 | Wrap up | Complexity, trade-offs, follow-ups |

## Phase 1: clarify (0–5 min)

Repeat the problem in your own words, then ask about the things that change the algorithm:

- "Can the array contain **negatives**? **Duplicates**?" These decide between sliding window and prefix sums, and whether you dedupe.
- "How large can **n** be?" This sets your target complexity ([The Pattern Picker](/dsa/playbook/pattern-picker)).
- "Is the input **sorted**? Can I modify it?"
- "What should I return if there's **no answer**: −1, empty, or an exception?"
- "Is there exactly one answer, or should I return any or all?"

Then **write a small example** with its expected output and get it confirmed. This takes 60 seconds, and it catches misreadings before they cost you 20 minutes.

> "So I'm given an unsorted array, possibly with negatives, n up to 10⁵, and I return the number of subarrays summing to k. For [1, 2, 3] and k = 3 the answer is 2: [1, 2] and [3]. Is that right?"

## Phase 2: approach (5–15 min)

**Say the brute force out loud first**, even if you already see something better. It proves you understand the problem and gives the interviewer something to anchor on.

> "The brute force tries every start and end and sums each subarray. That's O(n²) with a running sum. With n = 10⁵ that's 10¹⁰, which is too slow."

**Name the waste, then the fix.** This is the sentence interviewers love:

> "The brute force keeps re-adding the same elements. If I store prefix sums in a hash map, then for each position I just ask how many earlier prefixes equal current minus k. That's O(n) time and O(n) space."

**Get agreement before coding:** "Does that approach sound good, or would you like me to consider something else?" If the interviewer hints at a different direction, it's far cheaper to hear it now.

If you see two approaches, mention both with their trade-off ("sorting gives O(1) extra space but O(n log n) time; hashing gives O(n) time but O(n) space") and pick one.

## Phase 3: code (15–35 min)

- **Narrate intent, not syntax.** Say "Now I extend the window and update the count", not "for int i equals zero".
- **Use real names**: `left`, `right`, `windowSum`, `seen`. Avoid `a`, `b` and `tmp2`.
- **Pull out helpers** for fiddly pieces (`isValid(r, c)`, `neighbors(node)`). You can say "I'll write this helper at the end".
- **Write the template you know.** Every pattern in this course has one. A familiar skeleton is faster and safer than improvising.
- If you notice a bug mid-way, say so: "I'll come back to the empty-input case." Then do come back to it.

What "good code" means to an interviewer, in the order they notice it:

- **Correct**, including on unexpected inputs.
- **Efficient**.
- **Simple**: ten lines beats a hundred.
- **Readable**: no clever bit tricks without a comment.
- **Easy to change**: a helper or a small class beats copy-pasted blocks.

Use data structures generously. A tiny `record Interval(int start, int end)` reads better than `int[]` pairs, and it costs ten seconds to write.

Silence while you think is fine. Say "Let me think about the loop condition for a moment" and then think. A long unexplained silence is the thing to avoid.

## Phase 4: test (35–42 min)

Don't announce "I think it works." Show it:

1. **Trace your example** line by line, tracking the variables in a small table, just like the step-through visuals in these lessons.
2. **Edge cases**: empty input, one element, all equal, negatives, the answer at the very start or end, and duplicates.
3. When you find a bug, **say what's wrong before changing it**: "The window should shrink while the count exceeds k, not if, so this needs to be a while loop."

Finding and fixing your own bug is a **positive** signal. A bug the interviewer has to point out is a negative one.

## Phase 5: wrap up (42–45 min)

State the complexity **with the reason**:

> "Time is O(n): each index is added once and removed at most once. Space is O(k) for the count map."

Then offer one improvement or trade-off if there is one: "If the input were sorted I could drop the map and use two pointers." Interviewers often have a follow-up ready, and this sets it up nicely.

## When you're stuck

Being stuck happens to everyone in interviews. What matters is what you do next. Work through this list **out loud**:

1. **Go back to the brute force** and ask what it repeats ([A Framework for Any Problem](/dsa/start-here/problem-solving-framework)).
2. **Run the three reads**: constraints, input shape, signal words.
3. **Try a smaller example by hand** and watch what you naturally do; that's often the algorithm.
4. **Ask what the answer depends on**. If it depends on smaller versions of the same question, think DP or recursion.
5. **Ask for a hint**, specifically: "I'm deciding between a heap and sorting here; am I on the right track?" A targeted question shows reasoning; "I don't know" doesn't.

If time is short, **code the brute force cleanly** and explain the optimisation in words. A working O(n²) solution with a clear explanation of the O(n) one beats a broken O(n) attempt.

## If you've seen the problem before

Say so: "I've seen this one, I think it's solved with a monotonic stack." Interviewers ask questions to watch you reason. If you recite a memorised answer and they find out, it reads as dishonest. If you're upfront, it earns trust, and they'll either give you another problem or ask you to explain it properly anyway. *Cracking the Coding Interview* gives the same advice.

## Phrases that help

| Situation | Say |
| --- | --- |
| Starting | "Let me restate the problem and check a few constraints." |
| Proposing | "The brute force is…, which is O(…). It repeats…, so I can…" |
| Before coding | "Does this approach sound reasonable before I write it?" |
| Thinking | "Let me take a moment to think about the boundary here." |
| Unsure | "I'm not sure X holds. Let me check it on a small case." |
| Found a bug | "This fails when…, because…; the fix is…" |
| Stuck | "Here's what I've ruled out and why; would a hint about… be OK?" |
| Finishing | "Time is O(…) because…; space is O(…) for…" |

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">

- **Coding immediately**, with no clarifying questions and no example.
- **Jumping to the optimal solution** without stating the brute force, which makes it look memorised.
- **Long silent stretches.**
- **Arguing with a hint** instead of exploring it.
- **Saying "it works"** without a trace or edge cases.
- **Complexity without reasons**, or forgetting space (including the recursion stack).

</Callout>

## Interview questions

<QA q="What should you do if you realise halfway through coding that your approach is wrong?">
Stop and say so. Explain which case breaks it and why, and propose the fix or alternative before rewriting. Interviewers value catching your own mistake, and it's far better than silently patching it or finishing code you know is wrong. If time is short, explain the corrected approach clearly and code the parts that change.
</QA>

<QA q="Is it bad to ask the interviewer for a hint?">
No. Hints are expected, especially on harder problems. Ask targeted questions that show your reasoning, such as which of two approaches to pursue or whether a property holds, rather than asking for the answer. Then use the hint well: connect it to the problem out loud and move forward. The problem-solving score depends far more on what you do with a hint than on whether you needed one.
</QA>

<Practice task="Pick any medium problem from your log. Record yourself solving it in 40 minutes using the five phases. When you watch it back, what do you check?">

Check that you:

- clarified at least two constraints and confirmed an example before coding;
- stated the brute force and its complexity;
- named the waste and the fix;
- got agreement before coding.

Also check your narration. It should explain decisions rather than read code aloud, and no silence should run longer than about 30 seconds without a "let me think about…".

Finally, check that you traced an example and at least two edge cases without being asked, and that you stated time and space with reasons. Most people find their biggest gap is testing. Make it a fixed ritual.

</Practice>

<Takeaways>

- Interviewers score problem solving, coding, verification, communication and complexity, not just the final answer.
- Clarify, approach, code, test, wrap up: roughly 5, 10, 20, 7 and 3 minutes.
- Always say the brute force, then name its waste and the fix.
- Test unprompted: trace an example, then edge cases. Fixing your own bug is a plus.
- When stuck, think out loud, use the framework, and ask targeted hint questions.

</Takeaways>
